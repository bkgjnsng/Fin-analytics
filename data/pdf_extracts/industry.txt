Does an Industry Effect Exist for Initial
Public Offerings?
Aigbe Akhigbe
University of Akron
Stephen F. Borde∗
University of Central Florida
Ann Marie Whyte
University of Central Florida
Abstract
We examine the impact of initial public offerings (IPOs) on rival firms and find that the
valuation effects are insignificant. This insignificant reaction can be explained by offsetting
information and competitive effects. Significant positive information effects are associated
with IPOs in regulated industries and the first IPO in an industry following a period of dor-
mancy. Significant negative competitive effects are associated with larger IPOs in competitive
industries, those in relatively risky industries, those in high-performing industries, and those in
the technology sector. IPO firms that use the proceeds for debt repayment appear to represent
a more significant competitive threat to rival firms relative to IPO firms that use their proceeds
for other purposes.
Keywords: Initial public offering, IPO, intra-industry effects, competitive effects, information
effects

1. Introduction
The literature presents clear evidence that initial public offerings (IPOs) of equity
are underpriced. For example, Ibbotson and Ritter (1995) observe that IPO underpric-
ing for the period from 1960 to 1992 is about 15%, on average. However, existing
studies focus primarily on the valuation effects for the issuing firm. We examine
whether the announcement of an IPO also has industry-wide implications. We are
motivated to consider the issue by previous studies which have shown that firm-level
announcements have implications for rival firms. For example, Lang and Stulz (1992)
and Cheng and McDonald (1996) find that bankruptcy announcements have signif-
icant implications for rival firms. Similarly, Slovin, Sushka, and Poloncheck (1992)
document significant intra-industry effects for seasoned equity issues by commer-
cial and investment banks, Akhigbe, Madura, and Whyte (1997) for bond rating
downgrades resulting in significant negative effects for rivals, and Kohers (1999) for
dividend omissions. Other studies document insignificant intra-industry effects on
average. For example, Laux, Starks, and Yoon (1998) find that large dividend changes
have diverse effects within the same industry, resulting in insignificant average price
changes, and Hertzel (1991) finds insignificant announcement period abnormal re-
turns for rivals in response to stock repurchases. Similarly, Howe and Shen (1998)
find that dividend initiations do not contain an industry-wide component. Instead,
the information conveyed by dividend initiations is firm-specific, resulting in no
discernible intra-industry effect.
In this context, we test the hypothesis that an IPO may have significant intra-
industry implications. An IPO is likely to have industry-wide implications for at least
two reasons. First, an IPO could signal a change in the outlook for the industry as a
whole, resulting in significant valuation effects for rival firms.
1 Second, the IPO could
cause a reassessment of the competitive balance within the industry. Since the IPO
raises funds that the firm can use to expand and compete more effectively, the firm
could gain a competitive advantage over its rivals. Indeed, Slovin, Sushka, and Ferraro
(1995) find that rivals react negatively to the 107 IPOs in their sample. They conclude
that the negative reaction occurs because the IPO conveys unfavorable information
to the market regarding industry prospects. However, their primary focus is on rival
reaction to equity carve-outs, spinoffs, and asset sell-offs. Further, they do not explore
underlying differences in the characteristics of the IPO or the industry that could cause
the reaction of rivals to vary. We build on their work using a more comprehensive
sample to explicitly examine the factors that explain the diverse valuation effects of
rival firms.
We find that the valuation effects for rival firms are insignificant, seeming to
indicate that IPOs are purely firm-specific events with no implications for rivals.

However, we find that the insignificant reaction can be explained by the presence of
offsetting positive information and negative competitive effects. Significant positive
information effects are associated with IPOs in regulated industries and the first
IPO in the industry following a period of dormancy. Significant negative competitive
effects are associated with relatively large IPOs in highly competitive industries,
those in relatively risky industries, those in high-performing industries, and those in
the technology sector. IPO firms that use the proceeds for debt repayment appear to
represent a more significant competitive threat to rival firms relative to firms that use
their proceeds for other purposes.
2. Hypotheses
The impact of an IPO on rival firms may vary, depending on several factors.
First, an IPO could convey information that is relevant to the industry as a whole,
causing a revaluation of rival firms’ share prices (information effects). Second, an
IPO could alter the competitive balance within the industry, causing the market to
reassess the relative competitiveness of rival firms (competitive effects). Finally, an
IPO may have an insignificant impact on a sample of rivals, either because the event
is firm specific or has a diverse impact on rivals, resulting in insignificant effects on
average.
2.1. Information effects
Ritter (1991) argues that firms may choose to go public when investors are
“irrationally over-optimistic” about the future of certain industries. Thus, an IPO could
be construed as a favorable signal for the industry as a whole, causing rival firms’ share
prices to be revised upwards. Conversely, Slovin, Sushka, and Ferraro (1995) note that
managerial signaling models developed by Leland and Pyle (1977) and Titman and
Trueman (1986), among others, are built on the assumption that there is an adverse
selection problem associated with the decision to go public. Given that managers
of the IPO firm are presumed to be better informed than outside investors, outside
investors infer negative signals from the decision to go public. If the information is
pertinent to the industry as a whole, the share prices of rival firms should fall. The
following factors are examined to test for information effects.
Relative size of rival firms : Following Atiase (1985), we hypothesize that the
strength of the signal conveyed by an IPO is inversely related to the relative size of
rivals. Since smaller firms are not as closely followed by analysts, there is generally
less information available about smaller firms. Thus, the incremental information
conveyed by the IPO is likely to cause a more significant revaluation in the share
prices of relatively small rivals.
Regulation in the industry: Kohers (1999) argues that industries that are relatively
homogeneous may have a more pronounced reaction to intra-industry information
transfer. One measure of the degree of homogeneity is the presence of regulation.

Kohers notes that the constraints imposed by regulation result in less diversity across
firms, increasing the propensity of investors to draw industry-wide inferences from
firm-level events such as IPOs. Thus, we expect IPOs by firms in regulated industries
to have stronger information effects for rivals.
Stock exchange of IPO : The exchange on which shares of the IPO firm trade
may be an important determinant of rival valuation effects. One school of thought
suggests that NYSE-traded firms have a higher level of institutional ownership and
are generally more actively traded, causing investors to assign greater importance to
information related to such firms (Kohers, 1999). Another school of thought suggests
that the opposite may be true. This is based on the assertion that there is a greater
degree of information asymmetry regarding NASDAQ-traded firms, which could
cause the incremental information released by the IPO to be more valuable. Thus,
the relation between the stock exchange of the IPO and rival valuation effects is an
empirical question.
First IPO in the industry: Previous intra-industry studies (Akhigbe, Madura, and
Whyte, 1997) have shown that the first event is generally associated with the strongest
rival valuation effects. Thus, we expect that the first IPO in an industry following a
period of dormancy should have the strongest information effects. The advent of a
new IPO generates additional exposure, both for the IPO firm and the industry as a
whole. To the extent that an IPO conveys information that is relevant to the industry
as a whole, the first IPO should result in more pronounced valuation effects for rivals
relative to subsequent IPOs.
2.2. Competitive effects
The decision to go public could cause investors to reevaluate the competitive
position of the IPO firm relative to its rivals. Since the IPO increases the visibility of
the firm and raises funds that the firm can use to expand and compete more effectively,
the firm could gain a competitive advantage. These changes are likely to affect rivals
adversely. The following factors are examined to test for competitive effects.
Growth opportunities within the industry : The extent of growth opportunities
within the industry could affect the ability of a rival firm to respond to the com-
petitive threat of a newly public firm. Firms with relatively high Tobin ’s q ratios
are likely to have more investment opportunities than those with relatively low
q ratios (Lang, Stulz, and Walkling, 1991). In the context of the entire industry,
rivals with relatively low q ratios should have fewer growth prospects and may not
have the ability to respond to the competitive threat of a newly public firm with
greater resources. Consequently, they are more likely to be adversely affected by
an IPO.
Degree of concentration within the industry: We expect that rivals in less concen-
trated (more competitive) industries will experience more severe competitive effects
(Lang and Stulz, 1992). The additional capital raised by the IPO firm is more likely
to be a threat to rivals in industries with a relatively low degree of concentration.

Interaction of degree of concentration and IPO size : We expect that relatively
large IPOs in industries with a low degree of concentration (highly competitive in-
dustries) should have the strongest competitive effects. The greater the size of the
IPO, the more funds available to the firm and the greater the potential competi-
tive threat. This threat would be exacerbated in an industry that is already highly
competitive.
Use of proceeds: The proceeds generated by an IPO may be used for different
purposes including debt repayment, expansion, and acquisition financing. On one
hand, use of IPO proceeds for purposes such as financing an expansion could gen-
erate a stronger competitive advantage for the IPO firm. Alternatively, use of the
proceeds for debt repayment could enhance the firm ’s competitive position to the
extent that its debt burden is reduced. In this case, rivals may react more adversely to
the IPO.
Relative risk level of rival firms: Rivals with a relatively high level of risk could
be limited in their ability to challenge their competitors or respond appropriately to
the competitive threat. Thus, to the extent that the risk level of rivals is higher than
that of the IPO firm, the competitive effects should be stronger.
Prior performance of rival firms : Underperforming rivals are more likely to
be adversely affected by a new IPO, because they may not have the capacity to
respond appropriately to the additional competition. Alternatively, high-performing
rivals could lose because firms tend to enter industries with excess rents. As more
competition enters the industry, the market anticipates that excess rents will be re-
duced, causing high-performing rivals to react negatively.
Technology IPO: Kohers and Kohers (2000) find that high-tech targets are per-
ceived as relatively attractive investments because of the higher growth potential
of technology firms. In general, the high technology industry is characterized by a
high degree of competition, a rapidly changing environment, and a high level of un-
certainty. In this environment, the entrance of a newly public firm with additional
resources would place rivals at a competitive disadvantage, causing them to react
more adversely to an IPO.
Degree of similarity: Lang and Stulz (1992), Firth (1996), and Erwin and Miller
(1998) argue that the degree of similarity between a firm and its rivals may influence
the intra-industry effects. Thus, we hypothesize that rivals whose returns are relatively
highly correlated with the IPO firm are more likely to be impacted by the IPO.
2.3. Insignificant effects
Two interpretations of an insignificant impact on rivals are plausible. First, the
event is firm-specific with no corresponding implications for rivals. Evidence of
this type of firm-specific event is presented by Hertzel (1991) for stock repurchases
and Howe and Shen (1998) for dividend initiations. Second, the event could have
heterogeneous effects within the industry. In this case, the IPO could have industry-
wide implications that are being obscured by offsetting information and competitive

3.1. Assessing rival abnormal returns
We obtain abnormal returns (ARs) for rival firms using the standard event study
methodology. The ARs are assessed on both the filing date and the initial trading
date. Since the filing date is the date on which public information first becomes
available about the new competition, rivals are likely to react on the filing date.
Rivals could also react when the IPO first begins trading, since additional information
may be released during that period. Following Lang and Stulz (1992), we form an
equally-weighted portfolio of publicly traded rival firms that share the same Standard
Industrial Classification (SIC) code as the IPO firm on both the filing date and the
initial trading date (day 0). This procedure accounts for potential cross-sectional
correlation of returns in the industry. We use the returns of each rival portfolio to
estimate the market model parameters over the period (− 220 to − 20) relative to the
IPO filing (or trading) date and compute the ARs of each rival portfolio (p) and
for each day in the event period (− 11, + 11). We report cumulative abnormal returns
(CARs) over three different time intervals: day (0), days (0,+ 1), and days (+ 2, + 10).
We then compute the equally-weighted average of the CARs of the rival portfolios.
Following the methodology of Mikkelson and Partch (1988), we use the z-statistic to
test the statistical significance of the CARs
.

differing prior performance may be explained as follows. Since firms tend to enter
industries with excess rents, more competition within those industries could signal
excess rent reductions. This could have a more adverse effect on high-performing
rivals, because their high performance may be partially driven by excess rents. Thus,
one could argue that high-performing rivals have more to lose from the entrance of
a strong competitor, and low-performing rivals may benefit more from additional
information released by the IPO. Furthermore, high-performing rivals may already
have been privy to the information revealed by the IPO, resulting in poor-performing
rivals gaining more from new information than they lose from competitive effects.
Thus, prior performance may proxy for both a competitive and an information effect,
depending on the prior condition of the rival firm.
Table 5 shows the CARs for three cross-classifications of rival portfolios: CORR
and HI, Tobin’s Q and HI, and IPO size and HI. The CARs are insignificant for most
subgroups with the exception of rivals with a low Tobin’s Q and a low HI. The sig-
nificant negative CAR of 0.30% suggests that rivals in low growth industries that are
not highly concentrated (i.e. more competitive industries) could be at a disadvantage
when a competitor goes public. However, the t-statistic indicates no significant dif-
ference between the CARs of rivals at the two extremes: low Tobin ’s Q and low HI
compared to high Tobin’s Q and high HI.

Model 2 presents the results for factors related to competitive effects. It is again
worth noting that the coefficient estimates on the competitive factors are generally
negative and statistically significant, with the exception of GROWTH and CORR. The
degree of concentration (HI) is inversely related to rival valuation effects, consistent
with our hypothesis that rivals in less concentrated (more competitive) industries
experience more severe competitive effects. The results suggest that the additional
capital raised by the IPO firm is more likely to be a threat to rivals in industries with a
relatively low degree of concentration. When we interact the size of the IPO with the
degree of concentration in the industry (HISIZE), the reaction is also significant. The
negative coefficient estimate suggests that large IPOs in highly competitive industries
have the strongest competitive effects. A large IPO generates more funds for the newly
public firm, increasing the threat to rivals, and this threat appears to be exacerbated in
industries that are already highly competitive. The coefficient estimate of− 0.029 for
the USE variable is negative and significant at the 0.01 level. This suggests that when
IPO proceeds are used for debt repayment the firm could be more of a competitive
threat to rivals, perhaps because management may be preparing for a more aggressive
stance in the wake of a reduced debt burden. The coefficient estimate on RELRISK
(− 0.010) is negative and significant at the 0.01 level, suggesting that competitive
effects are more pronounced for higher risk rivals that may not have the flexibility to
respond to the competitive threat. The prior performance of rivals (PRIORPERF) is
inversely related to the reaction of rival firms. This is contrary to the expected reaction
and suggests that underperforming firms may have less to lose from the entrance of
a newly public competitor. The competitive effects are significant for IPOs in the
technology sector. The coefficient estimate on TECH is − 0.019, and is significant
at the 0.01 level, supporting the notion that technology rivals are more vulnerable
to competitive shocks, perhaps because of the rapidly changing, highly competitive
environment in which these firms operate. Finally, the CARs are positively related to
CORR and TIME.

We also estimate a simple regression model (not reported in the tables) with
the degree of underpricing of the IPO firm as the independent and rival CARs
from the initial trading date (as opposed to the filing date) as the dependent vari-
able. Since the underpricing is only apparent on the trading date, the CARs on
the filing date (which occurs well before the underpricing is evident) would not
be appropriate as the dependent variable on that date. We estimate the model in
an effort to test the hypothesis that IPOs that are significantly underpriced pro-
vide more information regarding industry prospects. The coefficient estimate on
the degree of underpricing is positive (0.0063) and significant (at the 0.05 level:
t = 2.25), suggesting that more information is released by highly underpriced
IPOs.

5. Conclusion
We examine a comprehensive sample of IPOs to determine whether IPOs have
implications for rival firms. We find that the average valuation effects for rival firms
are insignificant. The insignificant reaction does not indicate that IPOs are purely
firm-specific events with no implications for the industry as a whole. Instead, we
find that the insignificant reaction can be explained by the presence of offsetting pos-
itive information and negative competitive effects. Significant positive information
effects are associated with IPOs in regulated industries and the first IPO in the industry
following a period of dormancy. Significant negative competitive effects are asso-
ciated with relatively large IPOs in highly competitive industries, those in relatively
risky industries, those in better-performing industries, and those in the technology
sector. Firms that use the proceeds of the IPO for debt repayment appear to represent
a more significant competitive threat to rival firms relative to firms that use their
proceeds for other purposes.
