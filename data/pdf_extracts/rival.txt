Why Does an IPO Affect Rival Firms?
Matthew Spiegel and Heather Tookes
Y ale School of Management
IPO ﬁrms’ rivals tend to experience performance declines following an IPO in the industry.
Why? We estimate a dynamic structural oligopoly model to distinguish between alternative
theories that can explain an industry’s evolution post-IPO. We ﬁnd that most changes in
rivals’ performance are due to industry trends that also drive IPOs. However, we also
ﬁnd some “competitive” IPOs where the IPO enhances the IPO ﬁrm’s performance at the
expense of competitors. These ﬁndings help reconcile prior evidence of average performance
reductions of both IPO ﬁrms and their rivals with well-known cases in which ﬁrms have
beneﬁted from going public. ( JEL G30, G32)
Received October 19, 2016; editorial decision February 12, 2019 by Editor Andrew Karolyi.
Authors have furnished an Internet Appendix, which is available on the Oxford University
Press Web site next to the link to the ﬁnal published paper online.
Most research on initial public offerings (IPOs) has focused on the short- and
long-term performance of IPO ﬁrms, but there are striking patterns in the post-
IPO performance of rival ﬁrms as well. On average, entire industries see their
values and proﬁtability decline following the typical IPO. For example, Hsu,
Reed, and Rocholl (2010; HRR) report that public incumbents experience a
3.3% decline in sales growth and a 2.9% reduction in proﬁtability during each
of the 3 years following an IPO in the industry. Competitors’ also exhibit
negative abnormal stock returns near completed IPOs.
Why does performance decline among an IPO ﬁrm’s competitors? The vast
IPO literature offers many theories describing when the beneﬁts from going
public exceed its costs, which might help explain rivals’ post-IPO performance.
Three such possibilities are (1) a ﬁrm may undertake an IPO to lower its
ﬁnancing costs, making it a stronger competitor; (2) founders and may wish to
retire or sell off their holdings; and (3) an IPO may occur when there is a shock to
industry fundamentals, making the beneﬁts of remaining private less attractive.

Taking a “one-size-ﬁts-all” approach to explaining the patterns we observe
can limit our understanding of why changes occur in industry performance
following IPOs. Rather than try to determine which theory is “right,” we start
with the assumption that each one might hold for at least some IPOs. We then
conduct an event-by-event analysis of the evolution of the IPO ﬁrm’s industry
pre-and post-IPO to determine which theory best ﬁts the data for each individual
IPO. The results indicate that industry-wide changes explain rivals’ post-IPO
performance in the majority of cases. However, in 5%–10% of cases, a stronger
IPO ﬁrm with new access to cheaper public ﬁnancing is the most likely driver.
Understanding which theory best explains the data requires having some idea
as to how individual ﬁrms would have fared absent the IPO. In this paper, we use
a dynamic model of industry competition based on Spiegel and Tookes (2013a)
to provide a baseline. The model takes place in continuous time and generates
quarter-by-quarter performance forecasts for each ﬁrm in the industry. We map
differences between these forecasts and actual performance for a particular ﬁrm
or industry to hypotheses capable of explaining the causes and consequences
of IPOs. We then produce a frequency distribution of the applicability of each
theory across events.
In our application, the model creates parameter forecasts that are consistent
with a null hypothesis that all ﬁrms in an industry change over time at the same
rate, in proportion to their pre-IPO values. That is, if the proﬁtability parameter
a

its rivals. Our analysis indicates this is not the case. On average, forecast errors
for the IPO ﬁrm are no different from those of its industry peers. This supports
the hypothesis that IPOs foreshadow future industry changes rather than cause
them.

Some of the most important insights in this paper would be difﬁcult to obtain
using existing models. For example, many dynamic structural models evolve
from the real options literature. Notable papers include those of Aguerrevere
(2003, 2009), Novy-Marx (2007), and Carlson et al. (2014). In these dynamic
models, ﬁrms compete via Cournot competition by expanding their production
capacity of the market’s single homogenous good. Increasing capacity lets a
ﬁrm sell more of its product but also reduces the overall price. Each paper in this
area then considers a particular setting (competitive, symmetric oligopoly, etc.)
to analyze. In principle, it is possible to redeploy one of these models to examine
the going public decision, at least in cases where the IPO is used to expand the
ﬁrm’s operations. However, we think the model used in this paper offers a more
ﬂexible setting for understanding IPOs. For example, most industries have ﬁrms
that compete via the production of heterogenous goods and operate with varying
degrees of efﬁciency. The real options models impose a signiﬁcant degree of
symmetry among the competitors that would make adapting them to such setting
rather challenging. In contrast, our approach allows both heterogenous goods
and a multiplicity of operating efﬁciencies.
How well does this paper’s empirical model capture industry dynamics?
The appeal of the Spiegel and Tookes (2013a) framework is that it captures
ﬁrm heterogeneity in an n-ﬁrm industry and is easy to estimate; however, our
ﬁndings and conclusions are relevant only if the model captures meaningful
variation in observed changes to corporate proﬁts and values before and after
a rival’s IPO. We conduct out-of-sample tests of model performance and ﬁnd
strong support for the model. Period-ahead forecast tests indicate that, if you
have to restrict yourself to one or two independent variables, then the structural
model’s predicted value and proﬁt changes should always be among them. Most
of the time, if you are restricted to just one, it would be the structural model’s
forecast. These out-of-sample tests also provide a check against results that
are driven by misspeciﬁcation bias. Misspeciﬁed and over ﬁt models typically
yield predictions about the future that are little more than white noise. (See,
e.g., the Goyal and Welch (2008) critique of the equity premium literature.)
The out-of-sample tests show that our model better predicts future changes
in the industry than do model-free alternatives like AR processes as well
as those that employ variables seen in other papers in the IPO literature.

Additionally, Harvey and Collier (1977) misspeciﬁcation tests of the model
on the full Compustat database also indicate that the model forecasts are not
just noise. Overall, the model’s failure rate equals the size of the test plus
approximately 1%. If the model were perfect, the failure rate should equal the
test size. The main body of the paper discusses this in further detail.
Our forecast error tests can be thought of as a generalized differences-in-
differences (DD) test. In a DD analysis, one compares the difference in some
value between dates 0 and 1 across two groups. In essence, the model takes the
date 0 value as a “forecast” of the date 1 value. Conclusions are then drawn
based on these forecast errors across groups. In this study, the forecast at date
1 is not the date 0 value, but the value predicted by the dynamic model. The
errors are the difference between the model’s forecast and the realized date 1
value.

1.1 A comparison with the real options literature
Before proceeding, it is useful to discuss our choice of the Spiegel and
Tookes (2013a) framework, in light of the related literature on real options in
imperfectly competitive product market settings. The models in the real options
literature focus on Cournot homogenous goods markets in which ﬁrms decide
whether to expand production. The ﬁrms either have identical production and
expansion technologies or they compete in two-ﬁrm industries. For ﬁrms, the
relevant trade-off is that expansion lets a ﬁrm sell more but also reduces the price
of the good being sold and increases the cost of the capital that is employed.
The expansion decision depends on the competitive setting the model employs.
Aguerrevere (2003, 2009) consider putty-clay capacity choice problems in
a market with linear (Aguerrevere 2003) and nonlinear (Aguerrevere 2009)
demand. The analysis initially examines a monopolist’s behavior and then
considers an industry withn identical ﬁrms. Novy-Marx (2007) replaces the set
of identical n ﬁrms with a continuum. These ﬁrms produce an amount equal to
their current capacity and compete by altering that capacity. Like Aguerrevere’s
models, ﬁrms again share an identical production and expansion technology.
Novy-Marx (2007) does create a level of asymmetry by allowing for variation
in initial ﬁrm capacities. (However, if all the ﬁrms were to start at an identical
size, they would remain identical through time.) Carlson et al. (2014) consider

1.2 Model forecast errors
The empirical tests described in the next section use a combination of the
model’s estimated parameters and its forecast errors. Forecast errors compare
the model’s predicted evolution of the industry post-IPO with what actually
happens. These differences allow us to see the degree to which an event, in
this case an IPO, pushes the ﬁrms in an industry off the path one might have
otherwise predicted. As such, they are a way to characterize an event’s inﬂuence.
To obtain the forecast errors, we begin by estimating the model parameters
using data from the IPO ﬁrm’s rivals during the quarters surrounding the IPO
event. Based on these estimates, the model generates predictions regarding
future performance of both the IPO ﬁrm and its rivals. The difference between
these projections and each ﬁrm’s actual performance are the forecast errors.
Depending on the IPO ﬁrm’s reason for going public, certain systematic
differences in these errors should develop.

3.6 Model veriﬁcation and comparison with prior models
Given our reliance on model estimation errors to infer causality, it is useful to ask
how well the model ﬁts the post-IPO performance data. Does it produce results
superior to those of models that employ simple linear structures to explain
rivals’ post-IPO performance? The HRR, CH, and CL models all analyze post-
IPO data of rival ﬁrms using regressions unrestricted by a model. The next
sections reveal that the forecasts of this paper’s structural model are superior
in a number of dimensions to the independent variables that other papers use.
3.7 Out-of-sample tests
Dynamic structural models have the potential to advance beyond the limits of
static models by, in part, offering a way to predict future events. To see how the
model in this paper performs in real time, we conduct a set of out-of-sample
tests. In them, a periodt+ 1 forecast is made solely on the basis of data available
as of period t. These tests also allow one to check for model misspeciﬁcation.
A misspeciﬁed model may produce in-sample parameter estimates, which may
even be “statistically signiﬁcant.” However, out-of-sample, it will perform
poorly if model itself does not represent the true process under consideration. In
what follows, the tests compare how well the model estimated here does against
both an autoregressive model and forecasts based on variables that others have
used in the literature linking IPOs to an industry’s subsequent performance. As
will be seen, the dynamic model in this paper does quite well at explaining the
out-of-sample evolution of the data.

The period-ahead market share forecasts are generated from regressions of

level. This might be expected; as other developments are likely to affect the
industry by year 10 following an IPO.
In Table 9, we also ask how well the dynamic model performs compared to
the other variables in the literature. Relative to a dynamic model, constructing
a set of forecasts for a static model is challenging. A static model by design
combines the current period’s parameter and explanatory variables to produce
an expected value of the current period’s dependent variable. To work around
this problem, we take the following approach. For every period t, we use data
through period t− 1 to estimate regressions of proﬁtability and value (levels)
on the variables from HRR. We then interpret the period t− 1 residual as the
predicted change in value from period t− 1 to period t. The basic idea is that if
the HRR variables imply that actual proﬁtability in period t− 1 is higher than
predicted, we should expect proﬁtability to decrease in the next period (i.e., the
HRR residuals are expected to have a negative sign in period-ahead predictive
regressions). We construct similar predictions based on the measures proposed
by CL. The right-hand-side columns of Table 9 present the results. In the case of
value changes (panel A), the model-implied changes are the only statistically
signiﬁcant predictor. In the case of proﬁtability, the model-implied changes
as well as the changes implied by the HRR and CL variables are statistically
signiﬁcant, with the expected signs. These out-of-sample tests support our use
of the forecast errors to shed light on the forces driving rivals’ performance
post-IPO.

Harvey and Collier (1977) tests provide another way to see whether the
model’s estimates are driven by misspeciﬁcation errors. We conduct these tests
on the entire Compustat database, using data from 1995 to 2015. As in the
IPO tests, utilities and ﬁnancial ﬁrms are removed, as are industries with fewer
than 3 and more than 50 competitors. We test for model misspeciﬁcation across
ﬁrms in two ways. The ﬁrst tests follow the original Harvey and Collier (1977)
paper, in which the authors perform a t-test on recursive residuals. We estimate
the model using data to time t. We use these parameter estimates to create
and record forecast errors for time t+1. We then repeat this procedure, using
data up to time t+1 to create forecast errors for time t+2, and so on, through
to the end of the period covered by the data. If there is no misspeciﬁcation,
then setting the size of the test to, say, 5% should result in a rejection of the
model for about 5% of all ﬁrms. Generally, we ﬁnd that the rejection rate is only
about 2% larger than the test’s size.

4. Structural Forecasts: An Analog to Difference-in-Differences
A dynamic structural model like the one in this paper produces forecasts that
adjust for underlying attributes that may vary across ﬁrms and over time. The
resultant forecasts allow tests that are analogous to a difference-in-differences
(DD) type analysis, but can be used in settings in which traditional DD is
difﬁcult to implement. DD can be problematic when there are few appropriate
control ﬁrms, when the event of interest causes changes to occur gradually
(making event windows difﬁcult to deﬁne), or when pre-event data are missing
for some ﬁrms of interest. Beyond the analog to DD, the structural model also
generates parameter estimates that can further reﬁne the conclusions one draws
from the data.
In traditional DD, an outcome variable X within a population A undergoes a
treatment. Researchers compare the change in outcome variableX in population
A to the change in outcome variable X within a control population B, which
does not undergo the treatment. Assuming well-matched populations , the
difference in the changes in X across populations A and B can reasonably
be attributed to the treatment. DD is a powerful empirical tool; however, data
limitations can restrict its use in some settings. For example, ﬁnding same-
industry control ﬁrms may not be easy when there are only a handful of
ﬁrms from which to choose. The size of the available pool naturally limits the
number of attributes on which ﬁrms can be matched. Forced compromises then
may yield inadequate matches that generate false positives in the empirical
tests. This can happen when industry shifts, whether because of increasing
competition from a new entrant or evolution in consumer tastes, affect some
ﬁrms more intensely over time than others. A truly dynamic oligopoly model
offers researchers a way to incorporate potentially important heterogeneity
across ﬁrms by allowing changes in the outcome variable of interest to depend
on both a company’s characteristics and the time elapsed. In this way, dynamic

To clarify the beneﬁts of estimating a dynamic model, consider the following
analogy. Several horses are training for a series of races. A researcher develops
a model that uses the horses’ training and last-race times to forecast how they
will perform from race-to-race. After the model is estimated, a few new horses
arrive, begin the training protocol, and enter the race series. In addition to
the standard training protocol, these new horses are given a special feed that,
in reality, is useless. Using the model based on the data from the untreated
horses, forecasts of future performance are created for all of the horses. The
difference between the actual race times and the forecasts are the forecast
errors. If the feed is helpful, then the forecast error for the treated horses
should differ from those of the other horses in the race (i.e., they should
overperform).
How does a dynamic model help reduce the likelihood of a false positive? In
this example, one empirical complication might be that the new horses’ times
improve at faster rates. A DD model would typically forecast the time in race
n + 1 to be equal to the time in race n. A dynamic model would consider
the time in training. Improvement over time is expected, and a reasonably
well-estimated model should show that it occurs at a declining rate. In this
case, the DD model would generate a false positive because the feed only
goes to the horses that have not been in training for very long. A dynamic
model would adjust for time in training and indicate that the feed is not
helpful.
5. Related Work on Intraindustry Effects
While the IPO literature is voluminous (for surveys, see Ritter and Welch
2002; Ljungqvist 2007), we are aware of only three other articles—HRR, CH,
and CL—that explore empirically how a ﬁrm’s decision to go public affects
the values of other ﬁrms in its industry. In each case, the authors document
deteriorating rival ﬁrm performance post-IPO, and they point to a strong IPO
ﬁrm as the likely cause. Our results indicate that, in most cases, IPOs simply
presage changes in an industry. They do not induce them. This is consistent
with the fact that most ﬁrms conducting an IPO are small and stay small over
the next several years.

6. Conclusion
Our results indicate that rival ﬁrms incur losses in the majority of IPOs, and
that these losses are driven by industry trends. The IPO is just a portent of
those trends. The dynamic model used here reveals that most post-IPO changes
arise because proﬁts tend to get squeezed as consumers increasingly view
the industry’s products as less distinct, making it easier for one ﬁrm to steal
another’s customers. Of course, this is not true of all IPOs. In about 7% of all
cases, the dynamic model indicates that, the newly public ﬁrm does get stronger
post-IPO, leading to a deterioration in its rivals’ performance.
To verify that the estimated model is producing meaningful forecasts, we
test it out-of-sample. We ﬁnd that the model performs well and that when we
add additional variables from the prior literature, the model remains the most
important predictor of post-IPO changes in both proﬁtability and value.
While it is important to document the changes in rivals’ performance
following an IPO, traditional empirical approaches make the drivers of these
changes difﬁcult to identify. The structural model used here offers a way to
uncover the economic channels driving the changes in performance following

A.3 Quarterly Data Alignment (Note)
Firms within industries sometimes have heterogeneous ﬁscal end dates. To
construct industry market shares using quarterly data, one should align the
reported values. To do so, we implement the following smoothing rule. Where
the ﬁscal quarter end date does not equal the calendar quarter end date, we
use data from the last report date preceding the calendar quarter end as well
as the ﬁrst quarter following the quarter end. Fiscal quarters are transformed
to calendar quarters via weighting the consecutive ﬁscal quarter values by the
distance to the calendar quarter end date.
